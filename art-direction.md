# ART DIRECTION --- THE LAST FRONTIER

**Project:** Fictional NASA exoplanet exploration mission\
**Format:** Scroll-driven, immersive landing-page hero / short-form web
experience\
**Creative direction:** Cinematic realism × experimental editorial
design\
**Primary reference:** *Interstellar* (emotional scale, astronaut
presence, cosmic environments, restrained spectacle)

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1. Creative premise

A lone astronaut travels through deep space toward a newly discovered
exoplanet that may be capable of supporting life.

The experience follows the astronaut continuously: departing the
familiar, crossing an extraordinary cosmic threshold, entering an
unknown planetary system, and finally reaching the destination.

This is not a disaster-escape narrative. Earth is not presented as
doomed, and the mission is not framed as humanity's last chance. It is
an act of exploration driven by curiosity and the possibility of a
larger future.

**The website should make the audience feel the possibility of another
world before asking them to understand the science behind it.**

## 2. JTBD foundation

### Emotional job

> When humanity faces uncertainty about its future, I want to believe
> that there are still worlds worth discovering, so I can feel hope
> rather than helplessness about what lies ahead.

### Functional job

> Discover and evaluate potentially habitable worlds beyond our solar
> system.

### Social job

> Feel part of humanity's pursuit of something greater than ourselves.

### Emotional hierarchy

1.  **Awe** --- the scale and mystery of the universe.
2.  **Curiosity** --- the desire to know what exists beyond our
    understanding.
3.  **Courage** --- the willingness to cross into the unknown.
4.  **Hope** --- the possibility that there are still worlds worth
    discovering.

Supporting emotional jobs:

-   **Find hope:** hope against an apparently impossible future.
-   **Protect what matters:** love, responsibility, and fear of losing
    what is precious.
-   **Discover the unknown:** curiosity, wonder, and awe.
-   **Believe in a future:** optimism, purpose, and reassurance.
-   **Defy limitations:** courage, determination, and sacrifice.

The central emotional transition is:

**Awe → Curiosity → Courage → Hope**

The audience should feel small in the face of the universe, but hopeful
about what humanity can achieve.

## 3. Audience and communication objective

The audience is encountering a fictional NASA mission, not applying to
become an astronaut. The experience must:

-   Establish why the mission matters.
-   Make the scale and uncertainty of the journey tangible.
-   Communicate the search for a potentially habitable world.
-   Build emotional investment in the astronaut's progress.
-   Leave the audience with a sense of possibility and shared human
    ambition.

Do not turn the experience into a long science explainer. Use concise
narrative copy, with mission facts and scientific details appearing as
supporting editorial annotations.

## 4. Art-direction keywords

**Cinematic, vast, quiet, mysterious, human, tactile, atmospheric,
experimental, editorial, restrained, awe-inspiring.**

Avoid:

-   Generic SaaS landing-page layouts.
-   Neon cyberpunk palettes and excessive HUD graphics.
-   Constant motion without narrative purpose.
-   Overloaded dashboards and decorative data.
-   Cartoonish planets, game-like environments, or visibly artificial
    astronaut proportions.
-   Apocalyptic Earth imagery as the main emotional hook.
-   Claims that the fictional planet contains life when the mission only
    investigates habitability.

## 5. Visual language

### Space and environments

Aim for grounded, cinematic science-fiction realism:

-   Deep blacks with subtle star fields; space should retain large areas
    of darkness.
-   Physically plausible lighting, scale, atmospheric scattering, and
    restrained lens effects.
-   A strong sense of distance between the astronaut, spacecraft,
    celestial bodies, and camera.
-   Volumetric haze and particles used sparingly to reveal depth.
-   Planet surfaces and atmospheres should feel geologically and
    physically plausible, even when fictional.
-   Use a restrained palette: near-black, cold blue, desaturated cyan,
    muted silver, and occasional warm amber/orange from engines, stars,
    or reflected light.

### The astronaut

The astronaut is the emotional anchor and must remain visibly present
throughout the journey.

-   One lone astronaut in a believable, detailed EVA suit.
-   Preserve consistent suit design, silhouette, scale, lighting logic,
    and orientation across scenes.
-   The astronaut should feel small against the environment, never like
    a superhero.
-   Show the full body where possible; use readable silhouette and rim
    light to maintain visibility.
-   Movement should have inertia and believable zero-gravity drift.
    Avoid fast, weightless flailing.
-   The astronaut's movement should connect the scenes: drifting,
    rotating, being carried through the transition, stabilising,
    descending, and finally standing on the destination.
-   Do not let the astronaut disappear behind typography or become an
    incidental background speck.

### Typography and editorial treatment

Typography should contrast with the realism of the 3D environments.

-   Use oversized, expressive editorial headlines.
-   Combine a clean contemporary sans-serif with a distinctive display
    face; use a monospace sparingly for mission metadata.
-   Allow unconventional alignment, cropping, scale shifts, and
    asymmetric composition.
-   Use short statements rather than paragraphs.
-   Treat typography as part of the camera choreography: reveal, mask,
    drift, scale, or dissolve it in response to scroll.
-   Keep text readable and provide enough negative space around it.
-   Use small labels for mission name, coordinates, elapsed time,
    destination designation, and chapter markers. These are supporting
    details, not a dense interface.

### Texture and atmosphere

-   Subtle film grain, restrained chromatic aberration, fine noise, and
    gentle bloom.
-   Avoid heavy overlays that obscure the astronaut or planet.
-   Use imperfections as a tactile finish, not as a filter applied to
    everything.
-   Maintain contrast and legibility across desktop and mobile.

## 6. Experience structure --- scroll as the journey

The page should feel like an interactive short film. Scrolling controls
a continuous camera journey, while narrative text appears at deliberate
moments.

### Chapter 01 --- Departure

**Narrative question:** What lies beyond the only home we know?

-   Begin in deep space with Earth visible at a distance.
-   The astronaut is already present, drifting slowly in the foreground
    or midground.
-   As the user scrolls, Earth recedes and the astronaut moves deeper
    into space.
-   Reveal the mission title and a short opening line.

Suggested copy:

> For all our history, we have known only one home.

**Emotional job:** Awe and curiosity.

### Chapter 02 --- The unknown

**Narrative question:** How far can we go beyond what we know?

-   Introduce a wormhole or extraordinary gravitational phenomenon
    ahead.
-   Let light bend and the environment distort gradually.
-   The astronaut remains visible and is drawn toward the phenomenon.
-   Typography becomes more experimental as the familiar visual order
    breaks down.

Suggested copy:

> Somewhere beyond the familiar, another world may exist.

**Emotional job:** Curiosity and anticipation.

### Chapter 03 --- The crossing

**Narrative question:** What does it take to cross into the unknown?

-   The astronaut travels through a wormhole-like passage.
-   Use controlled perspective shifts, stretched light, particles, and
    gravitational distortion.
-   Make the transition feel immense and disorienting, but not chaotic.
-   Let the camera and astronaut move as one continuous sequence.

Suggested copy:

> Beyond distance. Beyond certainty. Beyond everything we know.

**Emotional job:** Courage and determination.

### Chapter 04 --- The reveal

**Narrative question:** What might another world look like?

-   Reveal the destination planet gradually: first as a light source or
    curved silhouette, then as a complete world.
-   Show atmosphere, cloud systems, surface colour, and scale.
-   Use a quiet pause after the intensity of the crossing.
-   Introduce a few concise, clearly fictional mission observations.

Suggested copy:

> A distant world. A possibility worth exploring.

**Emotional job:** Awe and discovery.

### Chapter 05 --- Arrival

**Narrative question:** Could this world support life?

-   Transition from orbit to atmospheric descent.
-   Keep the astronaut visible through the descent, with a clear sense
    of motion and scale.
-   Land on the surface; let the astronaut stabilise and take in the
    environment.
-   Show scientific investigation as the purpose of arrival: surveying,
    sampling, and evaluating conditions.
-   Do not imply that life has been found unless the fictional mission
    story explicitly establishes that discovery.

Suggested copy:

> We came looking for possibility.

**Emotional job:** Hope, responsibility, and wonder.

### Chapter 06 --- A new beginning

**Narrative question:** What could this discovery mean for us?

-   Let the astronaut stand against the planetary horizon.
-   Widen the composition and allow the environment to breathe.
-   Bring the typography back to a calmer, more legible editorial
    treatment.
-   End with the mission identity, a concise statement of purpose, and
    one clear CTA.

Suggested copy:

> The universe is larger than we imagined. So is what we can discover.

CTA direction: **EXPLORE THE MISSION** or **FOLLOW THE JOURNEY**

**Emotional job:** Hope and shared purpose.

## 7. Motion and interaction principles

Motion must serve narrative, spatial understanding, or emotional
emphasis.

-   Use scroll-driven animation for camera movement, scene transitions,
    and timed text reveals.
-   Prefer continuous transitions between chapters over a sequence of
    unrelated sections.
-   Maintain a clear sense of camera position and astronaut direction.
-   Use slow movement during moments of awe; increase intensity during
    the crossing; return to stillness at arrival.
-   Let text enter and exit with the scene rather than behaving like
    generic cards.
-   Use parallax only when it reinforces depth.
-   Avoid scroll hijacking that makes the page difficult to navigate.
-   Provide a reduced-motion experience and ensure the story remains
    understandable without animation.
-   Keep controls, navigation, and the final CTA accessible and usable.

## 8. Sound direction (optional)

If sound is included, it should be atmospheric and restrained:

-   Low-frequency ambience, subtle mechanical details, distant radio
    texture.
-   A gradual rise in intensity during the crossing.
-   A near-silent pause during the planetary reveal and arrival.
-   No continuous dramatic trailer music.
-   Sound must be opt-in or clearly controllable; the experience must
    work without it.

## 9. Technical direction for Claude Code

Treat the visual and the website as one authored experience, but build
them in deliberate stages.

### Recommended implementation approach

1.  **Inspect and select the visual foundation.** Explore vgpu examples
    and choose the best starting point for the primary environment.
    Candidate directions include black-hole/gravitational visuals,
    particle systems, atmosphere/clouds, Earth, or galaxy scenes. Verify
    the actual example and its implementation requirements before
    committing.
2.  **Build the core 3D scene first.** Establish the astronaut, camera,
    lighting, environment, scale, and performance before adding the full
    landing page.
3.  **Create a scroll narrative prototype.** Use temporary blocks or
    simple text markers to prove the chapter order, camera path, and
    scene transitions.
4.  **Add editorial typography.** Introduce the headline system, mission
    annotations, chapter copy, and CTA only after the visual journey
    works.
5.  **Polish motion and texture.** Add particles, atmospheric effects,
    grain, light distortion, and transitions selectively.
6.  **Optimise and test.** Check desktop and mobile, scroll performance,
    loading behaviour, accessibility, reduced motion, and fallback
    states.

### Engineering guardrails

-   Reuse the selected vgpu example's actual rendering approach and
    dependencies; do not invent APIs.
-   Keep the 3D scene, scroll orchestration, typography, and page UI
    modular.
-   Use scroll progress to drive a small number of intentional animation
    timelines.
-   Avoid creating a separate heavyweight 3D scene for every chapter if
    one continuous scene can achieve the story.
-   Keep the astronaut's appearance consistent. If the chosen technique
    cannot maintain a convincing astronaut, use a consistent 3D asset or
    a carefully art-directed alternative rather than generating
    unrelated versions per section.
-   Optimise geometry, texture sizes, particle counts, and shader
    complexity.
-   Provide a static or lightweight fallback if WebGPU is unavailable.
-   Do not sacrifice legibility, navigation, or responsive behaviour for
    visual effects.

## 10. Success criteria

The experience is successful when:

-   The astronaut remains a clear, continuous protagonist throughout the
    journey.
-   The audience can understand the mission's purpose without reading a
    wall of text.
-   Each scroll chapter advances the narrative and emotional arc.
-   The wormhole/cosmic crossing feels like a meaningful transition, not
    a decorative effect.
-   The planetary reveal creates a genuine moment of awe.
-   The ending leaves the audience with hope and curiosity rather than
    fear.
-   Typography feels art-directed and experimental while remaining
    readable.
-   The experience performs reliably and remains usable across devices.

## 11. Creative north star

> Make people feel the possibility of another world before asking them
> to understand the science behind it.

**Awe, followed by hope.**

The website is not selling a planet. It is inviting people to imagine a
future beyond the one they know.
